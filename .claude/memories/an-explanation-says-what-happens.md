# An explanation says what happens

An explanation written for the user says who does what, with a plain verb and everyday words. The noun phrases that the issues and the code are written in are hard for the user to read.

- Say which harness procreates, with that harness as the subject of the sentence. It is named the progenitor, per [[a-procreation-has-a-progenitor-a-copy-and-a-child]]: the progenitor procreates, not a procreation has a progenitor.
- Say that a harness attempted a task, or completed it, not that it has a finished attempt or that an attempt of it has finished.
- Name what the sentence is really about. A harness that improves itself with nothing to read makes a change that is a guess; the sentence is about its change, not about its child.
- Name each thing again, every time. No this, that, these or those points back at something said before, alone or before a noun: the score of the best harness on the holdout tasks, not that score; the holdout tasks, not those tasks or them. The grader of task A says whether the primogenitor completed task A, not each task's grader says whether it completed that task.
- Call a thing what it is, in the words the project has: the non-holdout tasks and the holdout tasks, not group A and group B. No letter and no made-up label stands for a thing.
- Say the literal thing. No figure of speech stands for what happens: the counts of the clade of the progenitor do not change and `cladenc` chooses the progenitor as often as before, not the progenitor escapes; `cladenc` adds tasks not completed to the clade, not `cladenc` closes the gap. A word used outside its plain meaning is not understood. That one such word was not understood is the user's; holding every figure of speech to it is mine.
- Take the user to know none of the project's words. The first time a reply uses one, define the word in everyday words: benchmark, task, grader, completed, held out, the holdout and non-holdout tasks, procreate, report and the rest. Say what a command does in plain words: `cladenc report` shows a table on the screen, not `cladenc report` prints. Add a small example in numbers, such as a benchmark of 100 tasks of which 60 are held out, and a sketch of what the screen shows; an example in numbers is easier to follow than definitions alone.
- Say what `cladenc` does in the place of the shorthand of the issues, or define the shorthand first. A progenitor reads about a task: `cladenc` gives the harness that procreates files about the work on that task. `cladenc` chooses by a task: `cladenc` looks at how the harnesses did on that task when it decides which harness procreates and which is the best. The non-holdout tasks: the tasks that are not held out.

Three of these are the user's rules. Demonstrative pronouns are left out: an explanation with many of them cannot be followed. A thing is called what it is: a label such as group A is an abstraction the user does not follow. And the project's words are defined: undefined, even task, held out and prints are not understood. Mine, not the user's: holding it, its and them to the rule on pronouns, the bullet on shorthand, and the example in numbers with the sketch of the screen.

**Why:** It is the user's rule. My reading of the reason: a phrase built as "the X of a Y" from the project's words has to be unpacked before it says anything, where a subject and a verb say it at once.

**How to apply:** Before sending an explanation, look for each "the X of a Y" built from the project's words, and for each "has a ...", and rewrite it as who does what. The scope is my reading, not the user's statement: replies to the user, and the part of an issue the user has to read to decide something. Whether the exact words stay in the rest of an issue is not settled.
